# F284 — the scroll bar

Raised 2026-11-12 by `curly gui`, which scrolls in three places and shows it in none: the request
tree, the response text area and a long list all move under the wheel with nothing on screen
saying there is more, or where in it you are.

- [ ] **F284** **A scroll bar.** (M; `lib/ui/controls.zm`. **The geometry and the gestures are
  done and tested; how a scrolled control acquires one is NOT decided** — see the measurement
  below, which rules out the obvious answer.)

  **Done: `controls.ScrollBar`, `thumb_of`, `scroll_bar_input`, `scroll_bar_widget`.** 16 unit
  tests, one bus check, three mutations.

  **A view of someone else's offset, not the owner of one.** The offset lives in the control that
  scrolls -- a `List`, a `Tree`, an `Entry` -- and a bar keeping its own copy is two numbers that
  can disagree about one thing. The bar holds only what nothing else does: whether a drag is in
  progress and where the thumb was taken hold of.

  **One `thumb_of`, used by the widget and by every gesture.** A bar drawn by one calculation and
  dragged by another jumps under the pointer; the test for it reads the thumb's box out of the
  laid tree and presses at its own middle. The drag keeps the point the thumb was grabbed at,
  which is the single thing a hand-written scrollbar gets wrong -- grab its middle and it should
  not leap so that its middle is under the pointer.

  **Not a tab stop**, which every platform agrees on, and the reason it publishes no value: the
  content takes the focus and its arrows scroll it. A reader walks rows that this layer publishes
  whether or not they are scrolled into view, so the bar is an affordance for the eye. The one
  check in the a11y fixture is that the node exists and `focus` does not offer it.

  A mutation worth keeping: removing the second clamp on the thumb's length -- the one that stops
  the twenty-point floor exceeding a shorter track -- was caught by **nothing**, because every
  test used a track of a hundred points. A bar beside a two-row list is fifteen points tall, which
  is now a test.

  ## Not done: how a control gets one, and why the obvious way is out

  A thumb's size is the view's share of the content, so a bar needs the **view extent** -- and
  nothing knows that until the layout has run. The obvious answer is to lay the window out, read
  the extent, and lay it out again with the bar; `curly gui` already does exactly that for an open
  dropdown.

  **Measured, and it is not affordable.** One layout pass of a tree in a 280x640 sidebar:

  | rows | one pass |
  | --- | --- |
  | 50 | 1.7 ms |
  | 200 | 5.6 ms |
  | 1000 | 33 ms |

  So a second unconditional pass doubles the cost of every frame, and at a thousand rows it is the
  frame. F270 got a regional repaint to **0.7-1.0 ms**; a layout pass is already five times that
  at 200 rows, which is its own finding and bigger than this task.

  Three ways out, none of them taken yet:

  1. **The control remembers the extent it was last told.** `tree_input` and `list_input` are
     handed `bounds` on every input, so a control could keep it. Wrong after a resize, which
     produces no input to the control -- the thumb would stay sized for the old window until the
     user touched it.
  2. **The control owns its bar in its own box**, laying out a row of viewport and bar. This is
     what every toolkit does, and it does not help: the widget is built *before* layout, so it
     still has no extent to size a thumb with.
  3. **A post-layout pass paints the bars**, as `widget.paint_focused` already paints the focus
     ring -- the proven pattern here for something that cannot be known at widget-build time, and
     the only one of the three that is exact and costs no second layout. A scroll node already
     carries its offset in the laid tree, so the pass has both extents. The gestures stay where
     they are; what moves is only the drawing.

  (3) looks right and is the reason this entry is open rather than ticked: it changes where a bar
  comes from, and shipping the widget half first would be a second way to draw one.
