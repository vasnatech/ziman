# F317 to F320 — a grid, a flow, a border, and the baseline the layout protocol never reported

Opened 2026-11-19, after a design conversation that began "lets add some layouts" and converged on
the one that is a capability rather than a convenience.

F317 and F318 shipped first and said flow and border were "better as their own item once the grid
has proved itself". It did, within the hour, so F319 and F320 are that item.

**Swing's `GridBagLayout` was the reference, and it gets more right than its reputation suggests.**
One layout for a whole form with no nesting; `fill` and `anchor` as separate questions; baseline
anchors; and `LINE_START`/`LINE_END`, added in Java 1.4 for exactly the Arabic and Hebrew work this
project has now committed to. What it gets wrong is that weights live on cells but mean tracks, the
magic numbers (`gridwidth = 0` means "all the rest"), the mutable shared constraints object, and
the absence of gutters. Keep the first list, fix the second.

Effort: **S** = a day or less, **M** = a few days.

---

- [x] **F317** **The measure protocol learns where a baseline sits.** (M; `lib/ui/widget.zm`,
  tests.)

  `measure(w, m) -> Size` could not express "my first line of text sits fourteen points down", so
  no layout could align on one. A label beside a text field was centred *by box* — which looks
  approximately right and is never right.

  `Measured { size, baseline }` and `measured(w, m)`, with `measure` kept as `measured(w, m).size`.
  **One recursion rather than two**: a separate `baseline_of` walking the tree again would be a
  second set of rules about what a column's first line is, free to disagree with the first, and the
  disagreement would show only in a form.

  **`Option<float>`, because most widgets have no baseline and that is a real answer.** A mark, a
  picture and a spacer have no text to sit on a line; a sentinel like `0.0` would be
  indistinguishable from a baseline at the very top of a box.

  A row reports the **greatest** of its children's baselines and a column its **first** — the two
  rules Swing and CSS both settled on. A row sharing one line has to report the lowest line any
  child wants, or the tallest text hangs below the box.

  **A box that fixes both axes reports none**, and that is the same statement it already makes
  about size: it has declared that its child's opinion does not matter, and a baseline is an
  opinion about where inside that size the text sits. `wide_of` fixes one axis and still descends —
  which is the spelling a form field uses, and the case that needs a baseline.

- [x] **F318** **A track-based grid.** (M; `lib/ui/widget.zm`, `lib/ui/a11y.zm`, a demo, tests.)

  The one arrangement nesting cannot express, because nesting cannot align across cells in
  *different rows*. Every form built on this library until now either nested stacks and gave up on
  alignment between groups, or hard-coded a label width.

  **Tracks are declared and cells are placed into them**, which is the whole difference from
  `GridBagLayout`. There a column's weight is the invisible *maximum* of the `weightx` its cells
  happen to carry, so two cells can disagree about a column and the resolution is a rule nobody
  remembers. Here a track is one thing that says one number. The track lists may be shorter than
  the grid — an undeclared track is `Natural` — so a quick form still declares nothing.

  `Share` is the same notion as `Flex`, so the library has one idea of weighted leftover rather
  than two. Gaps are first-class, where GridBag makes you put insets on every cell and watch them
  drift. `Column.Next` and `Span.Rest` are `RELATIVE` and `REMAINDER` **without the magic numbers**
  — a zero that meant "all of them" read as the opposite of what it did. And cells are immutable
  values, so GridBag's commonest bug, a shared constraints object somebody forgot to reset, cannot
  be written.

  **The sizing rules are written down rather than discovered**, which is where GridBag's
  reputation for unpredictability actually comes from: a spanning cell that does not fit puts its
  shortfall on the last `Natural` track it covers, and leftover with no `Share` track stays at the
  trailing edge — **not** centred, which is GridBag's one surprise that everybody meets once.

  **No role.** A layout grid is not tabular data; `controls.Table` is what earns `Role.Table`, and
  announcing a layout as a table is the oldest accessibility mistake on the web. Cells are laid in
  the order they were given, because a form's reading order is the author's to state and not the
  layout's.

  **A row is a coordinate here and not a node**, so a row background has nowhere to live. That is
  left to `controls.Table`, which has real rows. Recorded because it is the first thing someone
  wanting a banded form will ask.

  Seventeen exhaustive switches over `Widget` had to learn the variant and the compiler named every
  one. **`collect` is the exception and its own comment warned about it** — it tests cases with
  `if` rather than a switch, so a new variant compiles and paints silently wrong. A grid needs no
  clip and no special painting, so recursing is correct; it was checked by rendering rather than
  assumed, which is what that comment asks for.

  Verified by rendering a real form: labels trailing in a `Natural` column sized to the longest of
  them, fields sharing the leftover and all starting at the same x, and "Host" sitting on
  "api.example.com"'s own baseline rather than centred against its box.

- [x] **F319** **A flow that wraps.** (M; `lib/ui/widget.zm`, `lib/ui/a11y.zm`, a demo, tests.)

  The other thing a `Stack` cannot do: a row runs its children off the edge of its parent and
  nothing reflows. A toolbar that would rather wrap than hide commands, a bar of filter chips, a
  list of tags — none of them is expressible by nesting, which is why `FlowLayout` outlived most
  of Swing.

  **A variant and not a builder**, because wrapping needs the width it is given and `measure` is
  never given one. `Direction.Row` flows across and wraps downward; `Column` the other way.

  **`gap` and `line_gap` are separate numbers**, because they are different distances: items in a
  row sit closer together than rows of items do, and one number for both makes a bar of chips look
  either cramped or ventilated. `align` is `Leading`/`Centre`/`Trailing` — `FlowLayout`'s own
  constants went the same way in Java 1.4.

  **Each line sits on its own baseline**, from the same `Measured.baseline` F317 added, so a flow
  of mixed type aligns per line rather than on box centres.

  **At least one child per line, always.** A child wider than the whole container would otherwise
  put nothing on the line and loop for ever; it overflows instead, which is visible and finite.

  **And the wart is stated rather than hidden**: a wrapping container's height depends on its
  width, so `measure` reports *one line* and then the thing wraps into more.
  `FlowLayout.preferredLayoutSize` has exactly this behaviour and exactly this consequence. A test
  pins it, so the day it changes is a day somebody decided to change it.

- [x] **F320** **`border_of`, which is a function and not a layout.** (S; `lib/ui/widget.zm`,
  tests.)

  Swing's `BorderLayout` in twelve lines of pieces that already existed: a column of top, middle
  and bottom, with the middle a row of leading, centre and trailing. A new `Widget` variant would
  buy nothing that nesting does not already do, and saying so is the point — not every layout is a
  capability.

  **An absent region costs no gap**, which is the one detail that makes it worth writing down
  rather than leaving to each caller. `column_of(gap, [top, middle, bottom])` puts a gap either
  side of the middle whether or not the other two are there, so a border with no header gets a
  mysterious band of space at the top: `Nothing` measures zero but a gap does not. That is exactly
  the arithmetic every caller would get wrong once, and the test that pins it fails the moment the
  filter is removed.

  **Leading and trailing, not west and east**, for the fourth time in this programme. The vertical
  pair keep their plain names, because a page's top does not mirror in Arabic or Hebrew.
